Why a Slab Leak Hides for So Long

Many homes sit on a concrete foundation with supply lines, and sometimes drain lines, running through or beneath it. When one of those pipes fails, the water has nowhere obvious to go, so it soaks the soil, lifts flooring or simply drains away.

That is why the first signs are often indirect. A water bill that climbs, a floor that feels warm in one spot or the faint sound of running water at night are all typical. By the time a wet patch appears, the leak has often been active for a while.

What a Leaking Slab Tells You

Slab leaks tend to show up as a handful of recognizable clues.

A hot spot on the floor

A leaking hot water line warms the concrete above it. A patch that feels like a heating pad under your feet is a strong hint.

The meter moves with everything off

Close every fixture and watch the dial. If it spins, water is escaping somewhere in the house or the buried supply line.

Damp flooring or lifted tile

Carpet that feels wet from underneath, laminate that buckles and tile that cracks can all signal water pushing up from below.

Low pressure or a hiss

Weaker flow at the taps and a faint running sound from the floor at night often accompany a pressurized leak.

Floor Wet or Warm? Do This First

A slab leak is rarely a flood, but a few steps keep it from getting worse before help arrives.

1

Shut the main valve

Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the meter stops moving after that, the leak is inside the house side of the system.

2

Turn off the water heater

Cut power to an electric unit or set a gas unit to pilot. A hot line leak can drain the tank and leave it heating an empty shell.

3

Lift what you can

Move rugs, boxes and low furniture off the wet area. Keep towels out of floor vents, where water can wick into ductwork.

4

Note what you see

A photo of the wet area, the warm spot and the meter dial helps the technician decide where to listen first.

Do not drill or break concrete to look for the pipe. Cutting in the wrong spot can hit a second line and make repairs harder.

Soft Marsh Ground and What It Means for Buried Pipe

Suisun City sits beside Suisun Marsh, and local history says the town was built on marshy land. The railroad roadbed across the marsh settled 6 to 12 inches a year for decades until fill and drainage steadied it. Soil like that can keep moving at a small scale, and ground movement stresses the pipe that runs through or beneath a slab.

That does not mean any particular home has a problem. It does mean that if the pipe under your floor is old and your floor has settled or cracked, a leak there is a reasonable thing to rule out. A leak detection visit can confirm it before any concrete is touched.

Once the leak is pinpointed, the repair method depends on pipe condition, location and how many lines are involved.

When One Repair Is Enough and When It Is Not

A single leak in a line that is otherwise in good shape is a fair candidate for a spot repair. The pipe is exposed, the damaged section is cut out and replaced, and the concrete is closed up. Under the California Plumbing Code, which is built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), the replacement pipe and its joints must be approved for use under a slab.

Signs the pipe itself is the problem

If the opened section looks pitted, crusty or thin, the neighboring pipe is probably similar. A second leak a year later is common when the whole run has aged out.

Why rerouting is often the quieter choice

Running a new line through the attic or walls avoids breaking more floor. It is usually the plan when the old line runs under tile, cabinets or fixtures that you would rather not disturb.

For a house with repeated leaks, a whole-house repipe can end the cycle of patching. It is a larger job, but it replaces the unknowns with new pipe.

What Not to Try on a Slab Leak

Slab leaks attract some risky shortcuts. Here is how they compare with a planned repair.

Common shortcuts

  • Breaking concrete to hunt for the pipe
  • Pouring sealants into the supply line
  • Leaving the leak and just paying the bill
  • Hiding damp flooring under new carpet
  • Patching a drywall stain without finding the source

A planned repair

  • Pinpoint the leak before cutting
  • Pressure test each line to isolate the failure
  • Choose spot repair, reroute or repipe on evidence
  • Dry out the structure after the repair
  • Explain the price before work starts
