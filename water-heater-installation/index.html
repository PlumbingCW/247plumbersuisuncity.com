Planning a Water Heater Installation Before You Need One

Most installs happen because something changed: a remodel, an addition, a new build or a tank that is old enough to worry about. Planning ahead lets you choose the unit, the location and the timing instead of accepting whatever is available at midnight.

A good install starts with a few simple facts. Know the fuel type, the current tank size and where it sits, and tell the dispatcher what is changing.

From First Call to Hot Water

A scheduled install follows a clear order, so nothing surprises you on the day.

1

Describe the project

New construction, remodel, fuel switch or like-for-like upgrade. You get a clear price before work starts.

2

Confirm the site

The plumber confirms the gas line, power, venting path, floor and clearances. A pan and drain run are discussed if the unit sits above living space.

3

Install, connect and brace

The unit is set, plumbed and vented, then strapped to meet California bracing law. Gas and electrical connections are tested.

4

Start up and walk through

The tank is filled, purged and fired. You see how to set the temperature and where each shutoff is.

A little preparation keeps the visit short and the price accurate.

Local note

Bracing Is Part of Every Install in California

California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires all new and replacement water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped against falling or horizontal movement in an earthquake, following the California Plumbing Code. It applies to standard units of 120 gallons or less.

On your end, that means straps are included in the install, not an add-on. If you ever sell the house, the seller certifies compliance to the buyer, so a properly braced heater avoids a snag at closing. The same California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), sets how the flue or vent must be routed and how gas and water connections are made, so those details are part of the install and not extras.

Source: California Health and Safety Code section 19211.

Sizing for Your Household

Capacity depends on how many people live in the house and how they use hot water. Two adults rarely need what a family with several teenagers does.

Recovery rate matters as much as gallons. A tank that heats back up quickly can serve a bigger household than its size suggests, while a slow one runs out during back-to-back showers.

Mention the number of bathrooms, any large soaking tub and whether you run a dishwasher and washer at the same time. The plumber uses those details to recommend a unit that will not leave you short.

Garage, Closet or Attic: Where the Heater Goes

Location drives half the details of an install. The same unit needs different parts depending on where it sits.

Garage

Garage units often need protection from vehicle impact and a platform that lifts a gas burner off the floor. Ask how your plumber handles clearance.

Closet or utility room

Tight spaces limit the tank size and require a safe vent path. A drain pan with a run to a safe spot is the usual protection against slow leaks.

Attic

Attic installs add the weight of a full tank above ceilings. A pan and drain line are worth the extra work, because a leak up there can ruin a room.

Hard Water Planning Worth Doing Now

The City's 2025 water quality report shows hardness of 170 ppm, about 9.9 grains per gallon. On the common USGS scale that falls in the hard band, near the top of it.

A new install is the easiest moment to deal with that. Adding a softener or filtration system upstream protects the tank from scale from day one, and the plumber can place shutoffs and a flush valve while the walls are open.
