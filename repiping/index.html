How to Tell Whether Your House Is Ready for New Pipe

Pipes do not announce their end of life, but they leave clues. Repeated leaks in different spots, rust-colored water when you first open a tap and pressure that has slowly faded all point to wear throughout the system rather than one weak joint.

Pinholes are a common sign in copper, and flaking, rust and narrowing are common in galvanized steel. If you have had two or three repairs in a short time, a conversation about repiping is worth having. The California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), limits which supply materials may be used and calls for working shutoff valves and sound pressure control, so new pipe is chosen with those in mind.

Patterns That Usually Mean Repiping

No single sign settles it, but these patterns carry weight.

More than one leak in a year

A second and third failure in different places suggests the pipe is aging out. Fixing each one separately adds up.

Rust or brown water

Discolored water that clears after a minute usually comes from corroding pipe, especially in older galvanized lines.

Low flow in multiple fixtures

When every shower and tap has weaker flow, interior buildup may have narrowed the pipe.

Bluish-green stains or pinholes

Greenish residue at fittings and tiny sprays from copper walls signal corrosion that tends to continue.

Why Older Housing Stock Matters Here

Suisun City grew from 2,917 residents in 1970 to 22,686 in 1990, roughly eightfold. That means much of the housing stock dates from the late 1970s through the 1990s, and the original supply pipe in those homes is now decades old. Old Town has older buildings still.

Age alone does not condemn a pipe. But decades of use, combined with the water chemistry described below, make a repipe a sensible thing to price out if leaks begin to repeat. A leak detection visit can help sort a single fault from a systemic one.

Hard Water and the Pipes You Install Next

The City's 2025 water quality report measured Suisun City tap water at 170 ppm hardness, about 9.9 grains per gallon. On the common USGS scale, that is hard water and close to the top of that band.

Scale clings to the inside of metal pipe, to fixtures and to water heaters, and it can shorten the life of fittings. New pipe starts clean, but untreated hard water will still leave deposits at heaters and faucets. A water softener installed alongside a repipe protects the new system.

What a Whole-House Repipe Involves

The job follows a clear sequence, which can usually be done without moving out of the home.

1

Plan the routes

The plumber looks at the layout, the fixtures and the access points, then decides where new lines will run through walls, attic or crawlspace.

2

Open small access points

Drywall is cut only where needed to run pipe. Many routes use existing cavities, which keeps wall openings limited.

3

Run and connect the new pipe

New supply lines go in from the main to each fixture, and connections are made at the water heater, toilets and sinks.

4

Pressure test, then patch

The new system is pressure tested before anything is closed. After a pass, the openings are patched and ready for paint.

Repair a Few Leaks or Replace the Whole System

Neither choice is always right. This is how the two paths usually compare.

Keep repairing

  • Pipe is in decent shape apart from one failure
  • Leaks have been isolated, not repeating
  • Water quality and pressure are normal
  • Budget is limited right now
  • House may be sold soon

Plan a repipe

  • Leaks keep appearing in different places
  • Water is discolored or pressure is poor
  • Pipe material is known to fail with age
  • Walls will be open for a remodel anyway
  • You want to stop worrying about the next leak

Many owners start with a repair and then schedule a repipe if another leak follows.
