Emergency Water Heater Replacement: Do This While You Wait

Most failed tanks give a few minutes of warning before the water spreads. Work through these in order and then call the dispatch line.

1

Shut off the fuel

On a gas unit, turn the gas control to off. On an electric unit, switch off its breaker. Never leave a heater powered while the tank is empty or leaking.

2

Close the cold supply

Find the valve on the pipe feeding the top of the tank and turn it clockwise until it stops. This stops new water from filling the floor.

3

Protect what is nearby

Move boxes, tools and anything stored against the tank. Lay towels at the base and keep children and pets out of the area.

4

Do not drain a hot tank by hand

Water inside can scald for hours after the burner stops. Leave draining to the plumber unless the leak is serious and the water has cooled.

5

Gas smell? Leave first

If you smell gas, do not touch switches. Walk outside and call 911, then call us once you are clear of the house.

Once the shutoffs are done, call the 24/7 line and describe the tank: gas or electric, roughly how many gallons, and where it sits. That lets the plumber bring the right unit and fittings on the first trip.

How to Tell the Tank Is Finished

Some failures are loud. Others creep up for months before the tank gives out.

Water at the base

A puddle beneath the tank usually means the steel shell has corroded through. That is not a fixable leak, because the damage is inside the walls of the tank.

Rust-colored hot water

Brown or orange water from the hot side only points to a tank lining that is breaking down. Cold taps running clear confirm the problem is the heater.

Repeated repairs

A third part in a few seasons tells you the whole system is tired. Parts on an old tank cost money but do not slow the corrosion.

Loud rumbling and short supply

A thick layer of mineral sediment on the tank floor holds heat against the steel and cuts the amount of usable hot water.

Repair or Replace: How the Decision Usually Falls

A good plumber will tell you honestly when a repair still makes sense. These are the usual dividing lines.

Repair is worth a look when

  • The tank itself is dry and sound
  • A part such as a thermostat, element or thermocouple has failed
  • The unit is fairly young and has been flushed
  • The problem is a fitting or valve, not the shell

Replace when

  • Water is escaping from the tank body
  • The heater is well past a typical tank lifespan
  • Rust is showing in the hot water
  • Repair cost is close to the price of a new unit

If you are weighing the two, the water heater repair page covers what can be fixed and what cannot. When the tank itself is the leak, replacement is the only real answer.

Sizing the New Tank Without Guessing

Tank size follows the household, not the old label. A couple can often get by on a smaller tank, while a family with teenagers and two showers a morning needs more gallons and a faster recovery rate.

Replacing like for like is the simplest path, because the gas line, vent, drain pan and electrical feed already fit that size. Moving up or down a size is possible, but it can change the clearances and the venting, so the plumber checks the closet or garage before quoting a unit.

Fuel type matters too. Swapping gas for electric, or the reverse, adds line or wiring work. Say on the first call if you are open to a change, so the price you hear covers the right job.

What 170 ppm Hardness Does to a Tank

The City's 2025 water quality report lists hardness at 170 ppm, which works out to about 9.9 grains per gallon. On the common USGS scale that sits in the hard band, near the top of it.

Hard water drops calcium and magnesium onto the hottest surfaces in a house, and the bottom of a tank is the hottest. A heater that was never flushed collects a stony layer that forces the burner or element to work harder and shortens the life of the unit.

That is why a flush once a year and a working anode rod matter here. It is also a good reason to ask about water softener and filtration when the new tank goes in, so the replacement lasts longer than the last one.

What Replacement Day Looks Like

Most tank swaps follow the same path from the first call to hot water.

1

Call and describe the unit

Give the fuel type, tank size, location and any leak. You hear a clear price before work starts, and a same-day appointment is offered when available.

2

Shutoff and drain

The plumber closes gas or power and water, then drains and disconnects the old tank. Water lines, vent and gas connections are checked for wear.

3

Set, connect and brace

The new tank goes in with fresh connections, a safe vent path and straps or anchors to meet California bracing law.

4

Fill, test and walk through

The tank is filled, purged of air and fired, then tested for leaks and temperature. The old unit is taken away.

Local note

California Requires Water Heaters to Be Braced

California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires all new and replacement water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped against falling or horizontal movement in an earthquake, following the California Plumbing Code. This applies to standard units of 120 gallons or less.

For you that means a replacement is not finished until the tank is strapped properly. It also matters if you ever sell, because sellers certify compliance to the buyer, so a missing strap is a problem you inherit. The California Plumbing Code, which is built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), also governs the temperature and pressure relief valve and its discharge pipe, so the job is not done until that valve and its drain line are right.

Source: California Health and Safety Code section 19211.
