How to Repair a Dripping Tub Faucet in Suisun City, CA

A dripping tub faucet almost always means a worn washer, a damaged seat, or a tired cartridge inside the valve. Shut off the water, take the handle apart, identify which type you have, and replace the worn part. If the valve is corroded, hidden behind tile, or still drips after new parts, a plumber can finish the job.

By Suisun City 24/7 Plumbing | Published October 15, 2026 | Updated October 15, 2026

Why Tub Faucets Drip

Inside every faucet is a part that presses against a seat to stop the flow. Over time, that part wears out or the seat gets rough. Water then slips past, and you hear or see a drip from the spout or showerhead.

A small drip wastes more than most people expect, since it runs every hour of the day. It can also stain the tub and, if the leak is inside the wall, damage drywall and framing.

Identify Your Faucet Before You Buy Parts

The repair depends on the type. Look at the handles and how they move.

TypeHow it looksPart that usually fails
CompressionTwo separate handles that turn several timesRubber washer and brass seat
CartridgeOne handle or two, smooth quarter-turn actionCartridge or its O-rings

Ceramic discSingle handle, smooth lever feelDisc cylinder
Diverter tub spoutPull-up knob on the spoutDiverter gate or O-ring

Replacing a Washer or Cartridge

Set aside an hour and have a towel and a cup ready for screws.

1

Turn off the water

Shut the supply to the bathroom or the whole house. Open the tub faucet to bleed out pressure and plug the drain so no screw falls in.

2

Remove the handle and trim

Pry off the cap, unscrew the handle, and remove the escutcheon plate. Take photos as you go so you can rebuild it in the right order.

3

Pull the stem or cartridge

Use a wrench to loosen the bonnet nut on a compression stem. For a cartridge, remove the retaining clip or screw and pull the cartridge straight out with pliers.

4

Replace and reassemble

Install the new washer, seat, or cartridge, matching the old part exactly. Reassemble, turn the water on slowly, and check for leaks at the handle and spout.

Problems That Can Interrupt the Repair

Old tub valves rarely come apart as cleanly as the videos show.

Handles that will not budge

Mineral buildup can weld a handle to its stem. Work penetrating oil into the joint and let it sit before pulling. Heating the handle gently with a hair dryer can also help. Avoid prying hard against tile.

A seat that is chewed up

If new washers still leave a drip, the brass seat may be pitted. A seat wrench can remove and replace it, but a badly corroded seat is a sign the whole valve is worn.

No shutoff valves anywhere

Many homes have no shutoff at the tub. If you must shut off the whole house for each repair, think about adding local shutoffs. A plumber can handle that kind of plumbing installation while the wall is open.

Hard Water and Faucet Parts in Suisun City

The City's 2025 water quality report lists hardness at 170 ppm, about 9.9 grains per gallon. On the common USGS scale, that falls in the hard band, close to the top of it.

Hard water leaves mineral scale on the metal and rubber parts inside a valve. That scale roughens seats, stiffens cartridges, and can make a handle hard to remove. If your faucet drips again within a year or two, scale may be wearing out parts early. Rinse and inspect parts when you repair, and replace them with the exact match.

Repair It or Replace the Valve

A tired valve is not worth a third round of parts.

Repair the faucet when

  • The valve is a standard type with parts available
  • The drip started recently
  • The metal is intact with no heavy corrosion
  • The handle and trim come off easily

Replace the valve when

  • Seats are pitted and replacement parts do not seal
  • The body is cracked or weeping at the threads
  • Repeated fixes fail within months
  • You want to upgrade to a different style of fixture

Replacing a valve in a wall often means opening tile or an access panel. Our team handles faucet repair and replacement and gives a clear price before work starts.

After the Repair: Test and Watch

Turn the water back on slowly so air can escape and pressure builds gently. Open the faucet fully for a minute, then shut it off and watch the spout for at least ten minutes. Dry the handle and trim and check again for moisture, since a slow weep around the stem can take a few minutes to show.

Check the wall or ceiling near the tub over the next day or two. A fitting that was nudged during the repair may seep before it seals. Catching that early keeps a minor leak from soaking drywall.

Keep the old parts in a bag and note the brand and model on the escutcheon. When the faucet needs service again, you will know exactly what to buy or what to tell a plumber.

Tools and Supplies to Gather First

Having everything on hand keeps the bathroom from being out of service for a day.

  • ✓
    Adjustable wrench and pliers. For bonnet nuts and cartridge clips.
  • ✓
    Screwdrivers and a hex key set. Handles use different screws, and some are hidden under caps.
  • ✓
    Replacement part and the old one. Bring the worn part to the store and compare it side by side.
  • ✓
    Plumber's grease. A thin coat on O-rings helps new parts seal and turn smoothly.
  • ✓
    Towels and a cup. For water in the lines and loose screws near the drain.

Signs the Problem Is Inside the Wall

If the drip continues with the faucet off, if the wall near the tub feels soft, or if you see staining on a ceiling below, the leak may be in the supply pipe or a fitting behind the tile. New washers will not fix that.

Stop using the tub and get a plumber to look. Catching it early saves the wall and the framing behind it, and the repair may be as small as one fitting. Valve work inside a wall should follow the California Plumbing Code, so confirm an active CSLB license before the wall is opened.
